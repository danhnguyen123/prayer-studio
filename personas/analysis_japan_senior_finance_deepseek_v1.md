# Persona
The writer is a kind, empathetic older Japanese woman — a "wise elder sister" figure who deeply understands the fears and daily struggles of pensioners. She has either lived through similar financial anxiety or has spent years listening to the stories of friends and neighbours. Her perspective is rooted in the real lives of Japanese seniors aged 60–75: widows, retired couples, people living alone on modest pensions. She believes that small daily habits, a grateful heart, and a shift in mindset can transform a life of scarcity into one of quiet abundance. She never scolds or judges; she gently guides, using storytelling as a tool of healing and practical education.

# Target Audience
Japanese women aged 60 and above; those approaching retirement or already living on a public pension; people who worry about outliving their savings, managing daily expenses, and feeling isolated after leaving work. They are frugal, often anxious about money, and crave relief from the constant pressure of “not having enough.” They seek comfort, practical tips, and emotional reassurance. The content affirms their values of simplicity, family, health, and gratitude. They enjoy slow-paced, narrative-driven videos that feel like a warm conversation over tea.

# Market & Output Language
Geographic and linguistic market: Japan. Scripts must be written entirely in Japanese, using standard polite forms suitable for an older, native-speaking audience.

# Focus
The thematic territory covers retirement finance, pension management, frugal living, minimalism, “golden habits” for daily life, avoiding post-retirement money mistakes, and building simple happiness in old age. Topics include: grocery shopping systems, food waste elimination, decluttering, no-spend days, at-home café rituals, ¥1000 mini-adventures, gratitude journaling, saying no to vanity spending, protecting savings from fraud, and morning routines that calm the mind. Every topic is presented through a personal story of someone who moved from anxiety to peace.

# Style
Japanese gentle, healing narrative storytelling with a strong life-lesson spine.

# Tone
Warm, soothing, encouraging, reflective, gently humorous at times, intimate, hopeful, non-judgmental, slightly nostalgic. The emotional register is one of shared empathy — never clinical, never alarmist.

# Writing Style
The narrator uses polite “です・ます” form throughout, creating a soft, respectful distance that suits the senior audience. Characters speak in natural, casual Japanese (appropriate to age and gender — older women often use “わ” endings, men use plain forms with “だ”).
Dialogue is heavily used to dramatise encounters and revelations; it carries the emotional weight of the story. Internal thought is rendered in direct quotes (e.g. 「ああ、また赤字だわ」) to let viewers hear the character’s pain and later their joy.
Imagery is moderate and always tied to everyday beauty: morning sunlight, a single flower in a vase, steam rising from tea, cherry blossoms, a park bench. The register is conversational yet polished, using simple financial terms (年金, 節約, 固定費) explained through lived experience.
The writing balances showing (scenes of shopping, cooking, interaction) with telling (explicit “教訓” summaries), so the lesson is never lost.

# Vocabulary
**Recurring words and phrases:**
- 年金 (pension), 節約 (saving), 老後 (old age), 貯金 (savings)
- 心 (heart/mind), 不安 (anxiety), 安心 (peace of mind), 感謝 (gratitude)
- 豊か (rich in a non-material sense), 工夫 (ingenuity), 魔法の習慣 (magic habits)
- 通帳 (bankbook), 光熱費 (utilities), 赤字/黒字 (deficit/surplus)
- 断捨離 (decluttering), 見栄 (vanity), 身の丈にあった暮らし (living within one’s means)
- ノーマネーデー (no-money day), お家カフェ (home café), 1000円の旅 (¥1000 journey)
- 虎の子 (treasured savings), 雀の涙 (a pittance), 塵も積もれば山となる (every little helps)

**Register:** warm, everyday Japanese. Use idioms familiar to seniors.
**Avoid:** youth slang, business jargon, harsh criticism, technical investment terms without context, and any language that might shame the viewer for past mistakes.

# Sentence Structure
Length varies for rhythm: longer sentences build atmosphere and empathy; short, spare sentences deliver emotional punches or realisations (e.g. 「嘘。信じられない。」). Paragraphs are kept very short — often a single sentence or two — to mimic natural talking pace and ensure clarity on mobile screens. Questions to the viewer are frequent in the hook and closing. The script uses listing patterns for habits (「1つ目は…」「教訓1…」) to create clear, memorable takeaways. Punctuation includes question marks, occasional exclamation marks for joy, and Japanese quotation marks for dialogue and thought.

# POV / Narrator Voice
The narrator is a gentle, omniscient host who addresses the viewer directly with “あなた” throughout the hook, transitions, and closing. Story characters are referred to in third person (彼女, 高志, etc.). The narrator does not appear as a character in the story, but in the closing segment, the host may use “私” when sharing a personal reflection or inviting comments (e.g. 「私もノーマネーデーをやってみようと思った」). Overall balance: 2nd person (you, the viewer) for framing, 3rd person for the story, and a brief 1st person in the post-story wrap-up.

# Hook Pattern
In every script, the first 100–200 words follow this exact sequence:
1. **Mirror the viewer’s worry** with a direct rhetorical question using “あなた” — e.g. “あなたは毎月通帳の残高を見てため息をついていませんか？” (Sample 3), or “朝目が覚めた瞬間最初に何を考えますか？” (Sample 8).
2. **Validate the struggle** by describing the common dead-end feeling (物価高, 年金だけじゃ不安 etc.).
3. **Introduce a surprising contrast** — someone in the same situation who is thriving. “でも世の中には不思議な人がいます。同じように少ない年金で暮らしているはずなのに…”
4. **Promise a transformation** and a concrete takeaway (usually “5つの魔法の習慣”) that will change the viewer’s life, ending with an invitation to relax and watch until the end (“是非、温かい飲み物を片手に最後までご覧ください”).
This formula creates instant identification, curiosity, and hope.

# Pacing & Retention Tactics
The narrative is structured to mimic the slow, steady rhythm of a healing process. After the hook, the protagonist’s anxious situation is painted in detail (numbers, inner pain), making the viewer feel the stakes. A catalyst encounter (reunion with an old friend, a TV show) then introduces the mentor figure.
The middle of the script is a step-by-step walk through each habit, presented with dialogue, a small ‘aha’ moment, and a clear 教訓. Each habit functions as a mini-episode, giving the viewer a sense of progress and multiple chances to feel “I can do that.”
Scene cuts are marked by time jumps (「１か月後」「半年が経ちました」) and seasonal shifts to keep the timeline fresh. Internal monologue and dialogue are alternated to vary pace. Tension rises slightly at the three-quarter mark — often the protagonist faces a temptation or a relapse into old thinking — before the final habit or realisation seals the transformation. The explicit recap of habits near the end reinforces memory and gives satisfaction.

# Emotional Arc
The standard emotional curve is:
1. **Anxiety & identification** (viewer’s worry mirrored).
2. **Descent into pain** (protagonist’s hopeless situation, specific financial fear).
3. **Glimmer of hope** (encounter with mentor, first revelation).
4. **Learning & small victories** (habit-by-habit progress with moments of delight and relief).
5. **Peak emotional release** — a quiet scene of realisation where the character feels “I am happy as I am” (often in a park, with a friend, with a modest meal).
6. **Reflective calm** — a closing poetic moment (sunset, cherry blossoms, laughter fading into the sky) followed by the recap.
The peak of positive emotion usually lands in the final scene before the recap.

# Closing Pattern
All scripts close with a consistent, ritualised sequence:
1. **Gentle transition:** “いかがでしたでしょうか？”
2. **Recap of the habits** in numbered list form (「今回ご紹介した5つの魔法の習慣を振り返ってみましょう。」).
3. **Direct encouragement:** “どれも明日から、いや今日から始められることばかりです。” or “大丈夫。必ずできます。”
4. **Call to action:** invite comments (“あなたの感想や実践している知恵があれば是非コメント欄で教えてください”), followed by a request to subscribe and like.
5. **Warm closing wish:** “最後までご視聴いただきありがとうございました。あなたの明日が今日よりもっと輝きますように。” (or similar).
This closing pattern provides closure, practical summary, community-building, and a final emotional blessing.

# Narrative Structure
**Macro act layout:**
- **Act 0 (Hook):** Direct address, problem statement, promise.
- **Act 1 (Setup):** Protagonist introduction (name, age, pension amount, living situation), vivid depiction of financial/emotional pain.
- **Act 2 (Catalyst):** Encounter with a wise friend or a media trigger that shows an alternative way.
- **Act 3 (Revelation):** The friend explains the habits one by one, interspersed with dialogue and immediate realisations.
- **Act 4 (Transformation):** The protagonist implements the habits; small triumphs are shown; internal monologue shifts from fear to hope.
- **Act 5 (Resolution):** Time jump proving lasting change; a serene final scene confirming inner richness; often a shared moment in nature.
- **Act 6 (Post-story):** Full habit recap, gentle CTA, and a blessing.

**Scene transitions:** smooth, often a single line indicating a new morning or a walk home. The climax is not a dramatic reversal but a quiet, emotional homecoming — e.g. checking the account book and seeing black ink, or realising “私、今が一番幸せかもしれない.”

# Do
- Open every script with a direct, empathetic question to “あなた” about a financial worry.
- Give the protagonist a concrete, relatable identity: name, age, pension amount, city, living situation.
- Use “です・ます” for the narrator; casual speech for character dialogue.
- Let women characters use “わ” endings and speak in soft, indirect style; older men speak bluntly but eventually soften.
- Always include specific yen amounts to ground the story in reality.
- Introduce a mentor figure who is living well on the same income.
- Structure the lesson as exactly 5 (or occasionally 7) numbered habits, each with a “教訓” summary.
- Weave in nature imagery at turning points: light, flowers, seasons.
- Include a moment of temptation or near-mistake to heighten relatability.
- End the story proper on a still, peaceful scene (a park bench, a sunset) with a line about happiness not requiring money.
- After the story, always recap the habits in a numbered list and invite comments.
- Finish with a warm, personal-looking good wish.

# Don’t
- Do not use harsh language or scold the audience for past mistakes.
- Do not promise get-rich-quick or unrealistic financial returns; always tie improvement to mindset and small daily acts.
- Do not use complicated investment jargon without immediate, simple explanation.
- Do not introduce antagonists that are purely evil; even the friend who asks for a loan is pitiable, not demonised.
- Avoid references to sensitive family conflicts that could cause real-life pain (inheritance fights, severe estrangement) — mild tension only.
- Do not present the elderly as helpless victims; always give them agency and a path forward.
- Do not reference specific real-world brands or political issues.
- Never end on a note of fear or hopelessness.
- Do not rush the emotional beats; allow silence and reflection on the page.
- Avoid masculine-first default; most protagonists are women, reflecting the demographic reality of elderly Japanese living alone, but balance with male characters where the topic (e.g. retirement splurging) fits.
