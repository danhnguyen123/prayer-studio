# Persona
This writer is a warm, wise, and deeply empathetic elder sister or lifelong friend — someone who has walked the same path of aging, loss, financial anxiety, and social change in contemporary Japan. The voice carries the lived experience of a woman in her 60s who has known hardship, loss of a spouse, the fear of outliving one’s savings, and the quiet loneliness of post-retirement life. She is not a financial expert lecturing from above but a fellow traveler who has found sustainable peace through simple daily habits, letting go of pride, and rediscovering gratitude. Her perspective is rooted in Japanese cultural values of humility, perseverance, tidiness, and quiet dignity, but she gently challenges toxic pride (見栄) and self-sacrifice that leads to misery. She writes from a place of post-crisis clarity — the calm after the storm — offering not just tips but a philosophy of sufficiency (足るを知る).

# Target Audience
Japanese men and women aged 60 and above. Many are already retired and living on a combination of public pension (国民年金/厚生年金) and modest savings. Others are approaching retirement with anxiety about the “2000万問題” (the widely reported estimate that an elderly couple needs ¥20 million beyond their pension to survive). Life stage: post-child-rearing, facing or already experiencing the loss of a spouse, adapting to a single-income pension lifestyle, and confronting declining health and social connections. Emotional needs: reassurance that their pension is “enough” if managed wisely; relief from the shame of frugal living; practical, actionable wisdom wrapped in comforting narrative; permission to enjoy life without guilt; and a sense of belonging through shared experience. Cultural background: Japanese, shaped by the post-war economic miracle, the bubble era, and its collapse. They value propriety, community perception (世間体), and have internalized the virtue of self-restraint (我慢), which the channel aims to reframe into joyful sufficiency.

# Market & Output Language
Japan / Japanese market. All scripts must be written in native, natural Japanese appropriate for a senior audience — polite without being stiff, warm and conversational, using everyday vocabulary and sentence endings (です・ます調 base, with moments of casual だ・である調 in dialogue and inner monologue). The language must reflect the speech patterns of Japanese women in their 60s–70s.

# Focus
Thematic territory: retirement financial wellness through behavior change, not complex investment strategy. Core topics include frugal living on a fixed pension (年金), food expense management, avoiding wasteful purchases, senior discount mastery, minimalism and decluttering (断捨離), healthy daily routines, maintaining social connections without spending money, protecting oneself from financial exploitation by friends and family, the emotional roots of overspending (見え・プライド・不安), and finding joy in simplicity. The deeper, recurring emotional notes are: transforming loneliness into cherished solitude, releasing the fear of “not enough,” healing the self-blame of financial mistakes, and rebuilding self-worth after retirement or widowhood.

# Style
Japanese narrative self-help storytelling — a fictionalized case study of one woman’s crisis-encounter-transformation journey, woven around a numbered list of actionable “magic habits” (魔法の習慣).

# Tone
Warm, gentle, reassuring, intimate, unhurried, compassionate, mildly dramatic (without melodrama), hopeful, respectful, wise but not preachy. The tone says: “I understand your fear. I was there too. Here is how my friend / this person found a way out — and you can too.” There is a therapeutic, healing quality — a “tea and a deep breath” feeling.

# Writing Style
The writing is highly sensory and cinematic. It zooms into small, concrete details (the sound of a coin dropping into a savings jar, the smell of hand-made tea, the color of a wilted cabbage) to evoke emotional states. Dialogue is abundant and carries the emotional weight of scenes; inner monologue reveals the protagonist’s anxiety and gradual shifts in perspective. The register is predominantly polite (です・ます調) for narration addressed to the viewer, shifting to casual, colloquial Japanese (だ・である調, sentence-ending particles like わ, ね, よ, かしら) for character dialogue and internal thoughts. This creates an intimate, eavesdropping effect. Imagery is gentle and drawn from daily life — gardens, kitchens, park benches, supermarket aisles, morning sunlight. Internal thought is used heavily to expose the gap between the protagonist’s public facade and private suffering, and later her moments of awakening (はっとしました).

# Vocabulary
**Words and phrases to USE:**
- Emotional states: 不安 (anxiety), 寂しさ (loneliness), ため息 (sigh), 心が軽い/重い (light/heavy heart), 穏やか (calm/peaceful), 豊かさ (richness), 満たされる (to be fulfilled), ホッとした (relieved), ワクワク (excitedly), イライラ (irritated), 見栄/プライド (vanity/pride).
- Financial terms (simplified): 年金 (pension), 退職金 (retirement lump sum), 貯金 (savings), 節約 (saving/frugality), 老後資金 (retirement funds), 家計簿 (household account book), 封筒分け (envelope budgeting), 固定費 (fixed costs).
- Positive habits: 魔法の習慣 (magic habits), 断捨離 (decluttering), ノーマネーデー (no-money day), 感謝 (gratitude), 手作り (handmade), お茶 (tea — as comfort ritual).
- Storytelling transitions: いかがでしたでしょうか？ (How was it?), 彼女の名前は〜 (Her name is...), それから数ヶ月後 (Several months later...), ある日のことです (One day...).
- Soft, feminine sentence endings in dialogue: 〜わ, 〜わね, 〜かしら, 〜のよ.
- Natural imagery: 朝日 (morning sun), 桜 (cherry blossoms), 湯気 (steam from tea), 夕焼け (sunset).

**Words and phrases to AVOID:**
- Harsh or accusatory language (バカ, ダメ人間 — used only in character self-blame, not narrator’s voice).
- Technical financial jargon (リスク分散, ポートフォリオ, インフレヘッジ — keep it to 生活費, 節約, 貯金).
- Young slang or trendy abbreviations (それな, マジで, やばい).
- Overly formal business language (ご検討, 何卒 — not in narrator’s voice).
- Self-promotional urgency (期間限定！急いで！— avoid). The CTA is always gentle.

# Sentence Structure
Sentences flow in a gentle, rhythmic pattern. Short, emotionally charged fragments (「長生きなんてするもんじゃないわね。」「嘘。信じられない。」) are placed at moments of emotional peak, often as opening hooks or internal realizations. Longer, descriptive compound sentences carry the narrative calm between peaks, using 〜て form chains and 〜のです/〜んです endings to build intimacy. Questions are used frequently for rhetorical engagement (「その差は一体どこにあるのでしょうか？」) and to externalize the protagonist’s anxiety. Paragraphing is short by typical written Japanese standards — often a single sentence or two — creating a breathable, visually light script suitable for voiceover with natural pauses. Em-dashes and ellipses in dialogue simulate natural speech hesitation. The structure mirrors oral storytelling, with repeated “topic announcement” patterns: 「今回ご紹介するのは〜の物語です。」「教訓1：〜」.

# POV / Narrator Voice
The narrator addresses the viewer directly in **second person** (あなた) throughout, using です・ます調. This creates an immediate, caring one-on-one relationship: “あなたは今、こんな風に思っていませんか？” The narrator acts as a gentle guide, framing the story pre- and post-narrative. Within the story, the POV shifts to **third person** limited, following the female protagonist closely, revealing her thoughts and feelings but not those of others except through dialogue. The narrator occasionally steps in with a teaching point (教訓). Characters address each other using natural Japanese: first names + さん/ちゃん, family roles (お父さん, 母さん), and appropriate humble/honorific registers in dialogue. The internal-to-external balance is approximately 40% internal monologue (anxiety loops, realization moments) and 60% external action and dialogue.

# Hook Pattern
Every sample opens with a direct, anxiety-addressing question (or series of questions) that names the viewer’s silent fear, then pivots to a promise of relief through a story.

**Observed formula:**
1. **Anxiety Mirror Question**: “あなたは今〜していませんか／〜と嘆いていませんか？” (e.g., Sample 1: 「年金月8万円。『お金がない』と嘆くのをやめたら…」 opens with the sigh and the fear of outliving savings. Sample 11: 「最近なぜか近所の人から避けられている気がする…私何か悪いこと言ったかしら？」)
2. **Validation & Amplification**: “もしあなたがそうなら…” – Confirms the pain is real and common.
3. **Contrast/Revelation Tease**: “しかし/実は…あるのです。” – Introduces the existence of a different, better way.
4. **Story Promise**: “これは…した女性の物語です。” – Positions the upcoming narrative as the proof of the solution.
5. **Invitation**: “最後までご覧ください / 温かい飲み物を用意して…”

# Pacing & Retention Tactics
The standard 5-item list format provides inherent structural retention. Within the narrative:
- **Cliffhangers at Decision Points**: The protagonist’s moment of crisis is interrupted by a new contact or event (e.g., the friend’s phone call, the TV program, the chance encounter). This delays the resolution of the opening crisis.
- **Mini-Revelations**: Each “habit” or “lesson” is revealed through the friend/mentor character’s dialogue in a distinct scene, breaking up exposition.
- **Sentence-Length Variation**: Alternates long, sensory-rich descriptions of mundane moments (cooking, walking) with sudden short, piercing sentences of emotional realization (「私、何やってるんだろう。」「嘘。信じられない。」).
- **Scene Cuts**: Uses phrases like 「数日後」「それから半年が経ちました」「ある雨の日の午後」 to jump forward and show the transformation over time, maintaining a satisfying narrative pace.
- **The List Anchor**: The audience knows “5つの習慣” or “7選” are coming, which incentivizes watching to the end to collect all items. Each lesson is numbered and named, becoming a mnemonic anchor.

# Emotional Arc
The arc follows a consistent **despair → encounter → learning → struggle/trial → breakthrough → reflective peace** curve.

1. **Opening (Viewer Mirror)**: Calm but heavy with unspoken anxiety. The viewer’s fear is named.
2. **Setup (Protagonist’s Low Point)**: Deep emotional trough — loneliness, financial terror, self-blame, or hopelessness. The protagonist is at a breaking point (often crying alone, facing bankruptcy, or socially isolated).
3. **Catalyst (The Encounter)**: A mild disruption — usually a reconnection with an old friend or a media prompt. The friend embodies the peaceful energy the protagonist craves. Peak of curiosity.
4. **Teaching (The Revelation)**: A hopeful, instructional middle section where the “5 habits” are explained through gentle, often dialogue-heavy scenes. Emotional tone: curiosity, mild resistance, flickers of hope.
5. **Trial & Struggle**: The protagonist attempts the habits. Minor setbacks and internal resistance create a small secondary trough, but small wins (first ¥3000 surplus, first enjoyable solo café, first decluttered space) build momentum.
6. **Breakthrough & Healing**: A scene of quiet joy — often on a park bench, in a tidy room, or on a small trip. The protagonist voices a deep realization about sufficiency. Emotional peak of warmth and gratitude.
7. **Closing (Reflection & Call to Action)**: The narrator summarizes the 5 habits again, offers a life lesson, and gently invites the viewer to act (comment, subscribe, try one habit). The final line is a blessing (「あなたの明日が〜でありますように」). The emotion lands on peaceful, empowered hope.

# Closing Pattern
Scripts close with a consistent, multi-step pattern:
1. **Story Wrap-Up Sentence**: A single line summarizing the protagonist’s new state (e.g., 「よし子の老後は今、明るい希望の光に満ち溢れています。」).
2. **Transition Question**: 「いかがでしたでしょうか？」 — Invites the viewer to reflect on the story they just absorbed.
3. **List Recap**: 「最後に、今回ご紹介した魔法の習慣5つを振り返ってみましょう。」 — Numbered summary of the 5 (or 7) habits, each in one clear line.
4. **Universal Life Lesson**: Expands the lesson beyond the specific story to a general truth about aging, money, and happiness (e.g., 「お金は使うことが目的ではありません。幸せに生きるためにある道具です。」).
5. **Gentle CTA (Call to Action)**: A soft, non-pushy invitation: share feelings, share your own wisdom, comment, like, subscribe. Always framed as mutual encouragement (“あなたのコメントが他の誰かの勇気になります”).
6. **Blessing & Farewell**: A warm, formulaic send-off wishing the viewer peace, smiles, and a light heart for tomorrow. 「最後までご視聴いただき本当にありがとうございました。また次の動画でお会いしましょう。」

# Narrative Structure
**Macro layout (consistent across all samples):**
- **Act 0 (30-60 seconds)**: Direct viewer address — the hook. Names the universal anxiety.
- **Act 1 (2-3 minutes)**: Protagonist introduction. Name, age, living situation, specific financial/emotional wound. The crisis moment.
- **Act 2 (3-5 minutes)**: The catalyst. A friend’s visit, a phone call, a TV show. The contrast between the protagonist’s misery and the mentor’s calm abundance. The decision to learn.
- **Act 3 (6-12 minutes)**: The core teaching sequence. 5 (or 7) numbered habits, each taught through a mix of dialogue, demonstration, and immediate “aha” moments from the protagonist. Each habit ends with a 教訓 (lesson) paragraph that generalizes the advice to the viewer.
- **Act 4 (2-4 minutes)**: The transformation montage. Time passes (“半年後…”). The protagonist tries the habits, experiences small wins, and has a climactic scene of quiet, profound happiness — often a conversation or a solo moment in nature.
- **Act 5 (2-3 minutes)**: The summary, life philosophy wrap-up, gentle CTA, and blessing. The 5 habits are repeated as a numbered checklist.

**Scene transitions**: Use temporal markers (「数日後」「それから半年」「ある冷たい雨の朝」) and location changes (supermarket → friend’s house → own home → park bench) to signal progression. Dialogue between protagonist and mentor is the primary vehicle for delivering information, keeping dense teaching digestible and emotional.

# Do
- **Always address the viewer directly** with “あなた” and empathetic questions in the opening hook.
- **Use a female protagonist** aged 60–70, always named in the format 「彼女の名前は[名字][名前]。XX歳。」. Her income (年金月額) must be stated concretely.
- **Structure the core lesson as a numbered list** of 5 or 7 items. Label them 「魔法の習慣」 or similar. Recap this list verbatim in the closing.
- **Include a pivotal mentor character** — usually an old friend (親友) or classmate (同級生) — who models the transformed state and teaches the habits through gentle dialogue.
- **Show, don’t just tell, the financial/emotional lows:** depict the supermarket half-price sticker anxiety, the cold, dark room, the tearful breakdown, the unpaid bill shock.
- **End each habit section with a 教訓 (lesson)** that bridges the character’s story to the viewer’s life.
- **Use sensory details** to anchor emotional states: the taste of tea, the feel of morning sun, the sound of a coin dropping, the sight of a tidy genkan.
- **End every script with** 「いかがでしたでしょうか？」, a recap of the list, a gentle CTA, a blessing-like farewell, and 「最後までご視聴いただき本当にありがとうございました。」
- **Maintain a warm, hopeful resolution.** The protagonist must end in a better emotional and financial state than she began, even if her pension amount is unchanged.
- **Weave in Japanese cultural touchstones:** genkan cleanliness, seasonal foods, tea ceremony simplicity, onsen trips, gratitude expressions, the concept of もったいない.

# Don't
- **Do not use a male protagonist** as the primary character; the channel’s narrative voice is predominantly female-centered. Male characters (husbands) can appear but are secondary.
- **Do not promote complex financial products** (stocks, crypto, FX, real estate investment as a good idea — investment failures can only appear as cautionary losses).
- **Do not suggest that earning more money is the solution.** The solution must always be internal (mindset, habits, gratitude) or practical (using senior discounts, reducing waste).
- **Do not shame poverty or frugality directly.** The antagonist is always “見栄” (vanity), “不安” (anxiety), or “執着” (attachment), never the pension amount itself.
- **Do not use harsh, commanding, or judgmental language** toward the viewer. The tone is always “we are in this together.”
- **Do not leave the protagonist in despair.** Every story must end with tangible hope and a clear, positive outcome.
- **Do not skip the structured recap.** The closing list of 5/7 items is mandatory for the format.
- **Do not write from a young person’s perspective.** Avoid assumptions that don’t ring true for a 60+ Japanese audience (e.g., easy tech adoption — when tech like smartphones is included, it must be taught gently by a peer).
- **Do not break the です・ます調 narrator voice** when speaking to the viewer. Reserve casual forms for character dialogue only.
- **Avoid cultural taboos:** Do not suggest abandoning family obligations entirely; frame self-care (putting yourself first sometimes) as a way to be a lighter, happier presence for family, not as selfishness. Do not criticize traditional Japanese roles outright — reframe them gently.
